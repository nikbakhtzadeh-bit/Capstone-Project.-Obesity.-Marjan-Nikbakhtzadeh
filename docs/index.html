1 Introduction

1.1 Background

Obesity is a major public health concern associated with a combination of demographic, dietary, and lifestyle factors. Understanding how these factors are associated with different obesity levels is important for identifying behavioral and health-related patterns. Examining these relationships may contribute to a better understanding of the factors associated with obesity classification.

1.2 Research Question

How are demographic characteristics, dietary habits, and lifestyle factors associated with ordered obesity levels among records in the UCI dataset representing individuals from Mexico, Peru, and Colombia?

1.3 Research Objectives

The objectives of this study are to:

  1. Describe the distribution of obesity levels in the dataset.
  2. Examine demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics.
  3. Evaluate associations between age, gender, family history of overweight, dietary habits, physical activity, and technology use with ordered obesity levels.
  4. Compare ordinal logistic regression models to evaluate the contribution of different groups of predictors.
  5. Assess model assumptions and diagnostic results.

2 Methods

2.1 Data Source

The data used in this study were obtained from the UCI Machine Learning Repository from the dataset titled Estimation of Obesity Levels Based on Eating Habits and Physical Condition (Estimation of Obesity Levels Based on Eating Habits and Physical Condition, 2019).

The dataset contains 2,111 observations and 17 variables. It represents individuals from Mexico, Peru, and Colombia. Importantly, approximately 77% of the records were synthetically generated using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), while the remaining 23% were collected through an online survey (Estimation of Obesity Levels Based on Eating Habits and Physical Condition, 2019; Chawla et al., 2002). Therefore, the observations should not be interpreted as 2,111 independently recruited study participants.

The dataset is publicly available through the UCI Machine Learning Repository under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. This license permits reuse, sharing, and adaptation with appropriate attribution. No restricted data were used or published in this project.

2.2 Outcome Variable

The outcome variable is NObeyesdad, an ordered categorical variable with seven levels:

  1. Insufficient Weight
  2. Normal Weight
  3. Overweight Level I
  4. Overweight Level II
  5. Obesity Type I
  6. Obesity Type II
  7. Obesity Type III

These categories represent an ordered progression from insufficient weight to higher obesity classifications and were treated as an ordered factor in the ordinal logistic regression analyses.

2.3 Statistical Analysis

Ordinal logistic regression was used to examine the associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle factors and ordered obesity levels. The analysis was conducted using the cumulative link model approach implemented in the ordinal package in R (Christensen, 2026; R Core Team, 2026).

Four nested cumulative link models were considered:

  • M0: An intercept-only model, used as the baseline.
  • M1: Included demographic characteristics: age, gender, and family history of overweight.
  • M2: Extended M1 by adding dietary variables: frequent consumption of high-calorie food (FAVC), vegetable consumption (FCVC), number of main meals (NCP), consumption of food between meals (CAEC), daily water consumption (CH2O), and monitoring of calorie consumption (SCC).
  • M3: Extended M2 by adding physical activity (FAF) and time spent using technological devices (TUE).

The models were compared to examine whether adding groups of predictors improved model fit. Model fit was evaluated using log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and likelihood-ratio tests where appropriate.

Parameter estimates were interpreted using odds ratios and their confidence intervals. Model assumptions and diagnostic results were also examined.

2.4 Data Preparation

The dataset was imported into R using the readr package. Initial data inspection confirmed that the dataset contained 2,111 observations and 17 variables, with no missing values found.

The outcome variable, NObeyesdad, was converted into an ordered factor with seven levels, ranging from Insufficient Weight to Obesity Type III.

The variables Height and Weight were excluded from the primary regression models because body-size information is directly related to the construction of obesity categories. Including these variables could introduce target leakage and result in overly optimistic model performance.

3.2 Ordinal Logistic Regression Models

To investigate the associations between demographic characteristics, dietary habits, and lifestyle factors and obesity levels, cumulative link ordinal logistic regression models were fitted using the clm() function from the ordinal package.

Four nested models were specified to evaluate the incremental contribution of different groups of predictors. The outcome variable, NObeyesdad, was treated as an ordered categorical variable with seven levels.

  • M0 (Null model): Included no predictors.
  • M1 (Demographic model): Included age, gender, and family history of overweight.
  • M2 (Dietary model): Added dietary variables to the demographic predictors.
  • M3 (Expanded model): Further included physical activity and technology-use variables.

Model fit was compared using the log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and likelihood ratio tests for nested models.

3.2.1 Fitting the Ordinal Logistic Regression Models

Code 
# Load ordinal regression package
library(ordinal)

# M0: Null model
M0 <- clm(
  NObeyesdad ~ 1,
  data = obesity_data,
  link = "logit"
)

# M1: Demographic model
M1 <- clm(
  NObeyesdad ~ Age + Gender +
    family_history_with_overweight,
  data = obesity_data,
  link = "logit"
)

# M2: Demographic + dietary model
M2 <- clm(
  NObeyesdad ~ Age + Gender +
    family_history_with_overweight +
    FAVC + FCVC + NCP + CAEC + CH2O + SCC,
  data = obesity_data,
  link = "logit"
)

# M3: Expanded model
M3 <- clm(
  NObeyesdad ~ Age + Gender +
    family_history_with_overweight +
    FAVC + FCVC + NCP + CAEC + CH2O + SCC +
    FAF + TUE,
  data = obesity_data,
  link = "logit"
)

The four ordinal logistic regression models were compared using log-likelihood, AIC, and BIC. Lower AIC and BIC values indicate better relative model fit, while likelihood ratio tests were used to assess whether the addition of predictor groups significantly improved model fit.

3.2.3 Model Comparison

Four nested cumulative link ordinal logistic regression models were fitted to examine the associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics and obesity levels.

Model fit improved progressively as additional predictor groups were introduced. The AIC decreased from 8213.63 for the null model (M0) to 7530.33 for M1, 6969.15 for M2, and 6881.09 for M3. Similarly, the BIC decreased from 8247.56 for M0 to 7581.23 for M1, 7065.29 for M2, and 6988.53 for M3.

Among the four candidate models, M3 had the lowest AIC and BIC, indicating the best relative fit according to these criteria.

Interpretation of Regression Coefficients:

The expanded model (M3) identified several associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics and obesity levels. Family history of overweight showed a strong positive association with the ordinal outcome (OR = 9.55, 95% CI: 7.47–12.25, p < .001). Age (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05, p < .001) and frequent consumption of high-calorie foods (OR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.69–2.79, p < .001) were also positively associated with higher obesity categories.

Other significant associations were observed for FCVC, NCP, CAEC, and CH2O. Physical activity (FAF; OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.59–0.71, p < .001), technology use (TUE; OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70–0.91, p < .001), and monitoring calorie intake (SCC; OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.30–0.70, p < .001) showed negative associations with the ordinal outcome. Gender was not statistically significant in this model (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.73–1.02, p = .089).

However, these odds ratios are based on the proportional odds specification, which assumes common predictor effects across cumulative outcome thresholds. Since the diagnostic assessment indicated departures from this assumption for most predictors, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously as model-dependent associations rather than uniform effects across all obesity-level thresholds.

3.2.5 Assessment of the Proportional Odds Assumption

The proportional odds assumption was assessed using the nominal effects test implemented in the nominal_test() function. This test evaluates whether the effects of predictors are consistent across the cumulative splits of the ordinal outcome. Significant results provide evidence against the proportional odds assumption.

3.2.8 Model Diagnostics

The diagnostic assessment identified substantial violations of the proportional odds assumption in the expanded ordinal logistic regression model (M3). The nominal effects test provided evidence of non-proportional effects for several predictors, suggesting that their associations with obesity levels were not consistent across the cumulative outcome thresholds.

The scale effects test also identified significant scale effects for several predictors, indicating potential differences in the variability of the latent response in relation to these predictors.

The supplementary multicollinearity assessment showed that adjusted GVIF values were generally close to 1, with no evidence of substantial multicollinearity among the predictors.

Although M3 demonstrated the lowest AIC and BIC among the candidate models, the violation of the proportional odds assumption limits the interpretation of its coefficients as common effects across all cumulative outcome thresholds. Therefore, M3 should be considered an exploratory reference model rather than a fully adequate proportional odds model.

4 Discussion

This study examined the associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics and ordered obesity levels in the UCI dataset using cumulative link ordinal regression models.

The initial model comparison showed progressive improvements in model fit as demographic, dietary, and lifestyle predictors were added. Among the four candidate models, M3 had the lowest AIC and BIC, indicating the best relative fit among the models examined. However, improved model fit does not necessarily indicate that all model assumptions were satisfied.

Diagnostic analyses revealed substantial departures from the proportional odds assumption. The nominal effects test indicated non-proportional effects for several predictors, suggesting that their associations with obesity categories may differ across cumulative outcome thresholds. In addition, the scale effects test identified significant scale effects for most predictors, suggesting potential differences in the variability of the latent response in relation to several predictors. These findings indicate that the standard proportional odds model may not fully capture the relationships between the predictors and the ordered obesity outcome. Nevertheless, the supplementary VIF assessment indicated no substantial multicollinearity among the predictors.

Overall, the findings suggest that demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics were associated with obesity classification in the analyzed dataset. However, because of the diagnostic limitations of the expanded model, its coefficient estimates and odds ratios should be interpreted cautiously. Given the synthetic component of the dataset and the observational nature of the data, the findings should be considered exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships or generalized directly to the broader population.

4.1 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings.

First, the dataset contains both survey-based and synthetically generated observations. According to the UCI Machine Learning Repository, approximately 77% of the records were generated using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), while approximately 23% were collected through an online survey. Therefore, the dataset may not represent the broader population, and the findings should not be generalized without caution.

Second, diagnostic analyses identified substantial violations of the proportional odds assumption in the expanded ordinal logistic regression model. The nominal effects test indicated non-proportional effects for several predictors, suggesting that their associations with obesity categories may differ across cumulative outcome thresholds. In addition, the scale effects test identified significant scale effects for most predictors, suggesting that the variability of the latent response may differ in relation to several predictors. Although M3 showed the best relative fit according to AIC and BIC among the four candidate models, these diagnostic findings limit the interpretation of its coefficient estimates. Nevertheless, the supplementary VIF assessment did not indicate substantial multicollinearity among the predictors.

Third, some categorical predictor levels had very small frequencies. For example, the CALC variable contained only one observation in the Always category. Therefore, CALC was excluded from the primary dietary model to reduce potential estimation instability. Other variables, including SMOKE and MTRANS, were also not included in the final expanded model.

Finally, the observational nature of the dataset does not allow causal conclusions. The reported associations describe patterns within the available data and do not establish that specific dietary or lifestyle characteristics cause changes in obesity levels.

The substantial proportion of synthetically generated records may also affect the estimated associations and statistical uncertainty. Therefore, the reported p-values and confidence intervals should be interpreted cautiously and may not reflect uncertainty in a representative population sample.

5 Conclusion

This study examined the associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics and ordered obesity levels using ordinal logistic regression. Family history of overweight, age, dietary characteristics, physical activity, and technology use were associated with obesity categories in the analyzed dataset.

Among the four candidate models, M3 provided the best relative fit based on AIC and BIC. However, diagnostic analyses revealed substantial violations of the proportional odds assumption and significant scale effects for most predictors, limiting the interpretation of its estimates. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating model assumptions and data characteristics when analyzing ordinal outcomes.

Overall, the results provide an exploratory assessment of factors associated with obesity levels in the UCI dataset. Further research using representative population-based data and statistical models that accommodate non-proportional effects and scale heterogeneity is needed to obtain more reliable estimates of these associations.

# Introduction

## Background

Obesity is a major public health concern associated with a combination of demographic, dietary, and lifestyle factors. Understanding how these factors are associated with different obesity levels is important for identifying behavioral and health-related patterns. Examining these relationships may contribute to a better understanding of the factors associated with obesity classification.

## Research Question

How are demographic characteristics, dietary habits, and lifestyle factors associated with ordered obesity levels among records in the UCI dataset representing individuals from Mexico, Peru, and Colombia?

## Research Objectives

The objectives of this study are to:

1. Describe the distribution of obesity levels in the dataset.
2. Examine demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics.
3. Evaluate associations between age, gender, family history of overweight, dietary habits, physical activity, and technology use with ordered obesity levels.
4. Compare ordinal logistic regression models to evaluate the contribution of different groups of predictors.
5. Assess model assumptions and diagnostic results.

# Methods

## Data Source

The data used in this study were obtained from the UCI Machine Learning Repository from the dataset titled *Estimation of Obesity Levels Based on Eating Habits and Physical Condition* (Estimation of Obesity Levels Based on Eating Habits and Physical Condition, 2019).

The dataset contains 2,111 observations and 17 variables. It represents individuals from Mexico, Peru, and Colombia. Importantly, approximately 77% of the records were synthetically generated using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), while the remaining 23% were collected through an online survey (Estimation of Obesity Levels Based on Eating Habits and Physical Condition, 2019; Chawla et al., 2002). Therefore, the observations should not be interpreted as 2,111 independently recruited study participants.

The dataset is publicly available through the UCI Machine Learning Repository under the [Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). This license permits reuse, sharing, and adaptation with appropriate attribution. No restricted data were used or published in this project.

## Outcome Variable

The outcome variable is `NObeyesdad`, an ordered categorical variable with seven levels:

1. Insufficient Weight
2. Normal Weight
3. Overweight Level I
4. Overweight Level II
5. Obesity Type I
6. Obesity Type II
7. Obesity Type III

These categories represent an ordered progression from insufficient weight to higher obesity classifications and were treated as an ordered factor in the ordinal logistic regression analyses.

## Statistical Analysis

Ordinal logistic regression was used to examine the associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle factors and ordered obesity levels. The analysis was conducted using the cumulative link model approach implemented in the `ordinal` package in R (Christensen, 2026; R Core Team, 2026).

Four nested cumulative link models were considered:

- **M0:** An intercept-only model, used as the baseline.
- **M1:** Included demographic characteristics: age, gender, and family history of overweight.
- **M2:** Extended M1 by adding dietary variables: frequent consumption of high-calorie food (FAVC), vegetable consumption (FCVC), number of main meals (NCP), consumption of food between meals (CAEC), daily water consumption (CH2O), and monitoring of calorie consumption (SCC).
- **M3:** Extended M2 by adding physical activity (FAF) and time spent using technological devices (TUE).

The models were compared to examine whether adding groups of predictors improved model fit. Model fit was evaluated using log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and likelihood-ratio tests where appropriate.

Parameter estimates were interpreted using odds ratios and their confidence intervals. Model assumptions and diagnostic results were also examined.

## Data Preparation

The dataset was imported into R using the `readr` package. Initial data inspection confirmed that the dataset contained 2,111 observations and 17 variables, with no missing values found.

The outcome variable, `NObeyesdad`, was converted into an ordered factor with seven levels, ranging from Insufficient Weight to Obesity Type III.

The variables `Height` and `Weight` were excluded from the primary regression models because body-size information is directly related to the construction of obesity categories. Including these variables could introduce target leakage and result in overly optimistic model performance.

## Ordinal Logistic Regression Models

To investigate the associations between demographic characteristics, dietary habits, and lifestyle factors and obesity levels, cumulative link ordinal logistic regression models were fitted using the `clm()` function from the `ordinal` package.

Four nested models were specified to evaluate the incremental contribution of different groups of predictors. The outcome variable, `NObeyesdad`, was treated as an ordered categorical variable with seven levels.

- **M0 (Null model):** Included no predictors.
- **M1 (Demographic model):** Included age, gender, and family history of overweight.
- **M2 (Dietary model):** Added dietary variables to the demographic predictors.
- **M3 (Expanded model):** Further included physical activity and technology-use variables.

Model fit was compared using the log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and likelihood ratio tests for nested models.

### Fitting the Ordinal Logistic Regression Models

```{r}

  data = obesity_data,

```

The four ordinal logistic regression models were compared using log-likelihood, AIC, and BIC. Lower AIC and BIC values indicate better relative model fit, while likelihood ratio tests were used to assess whether the addition of predictor groups significantly improved model fit.

Interpretation of Regression Coefficients:

The expanded model (M3) identified several associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics and obesity levels. Family history of overweight showed a strong positive association with the ordinal outcome (OR = 9.55, 95% CI: 7.47–12.25, p < .001). Age (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05, p < .001) and frequent consumption of high-calorie foods (OR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.69–2.79, p < .001) were also positively associated with higher obesity categories. 

Other significant associations were observed for FCVC, NCP, CAEC, and CH2O. Physical activity (FAF; OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.59–0.71, p < .001), technology use (TUE; OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70–0.91, p < .001), and monitoring calorie intake (SCC; OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.30–0.70, p < .001) showed negative associations with the ordinal outcome. Gender was not statistically significant in this model (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.73–1.02, p = .089).

However, these odds ratios are based on the proportional odds specification, which assumes common predictor effects across cumulative outcome thresholds. Since the diagnostic assessment indicated departures from this assumption for most predictors, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously as model-dependent associations rather than uniform effects across all obesity-level thresholds.

### Assessment of the Proportional Odds Assumption

The proportional odds assumption was assessed using the nominal effects test implemented in the `nominal_test()` function. This test evaluates whether the effects of predictors are consistent across the cumulative splits of the ordinal outcome. Significant results provide evidence against the proportional odds assumption.

```{r}
# Test the proportional odds assumption
PO_test <- nominal_test(M3)

PO_test
```

The proportional odds assumption was evaluated using the nominal effects test (`nominal_test`). The results indicated significant departures from the proportional odds assumption for most predictors, including Gender, family history of overweight, FAVC, FCVC, NCP, CAEC, CH2O, SCC, FAF, and TUE (all p < 0.05). These findings suggest that the effects of several predictors may vary across the cumulative thresholds of obesity categories. Consequently, the coefficient estimates and odds ratios from the proportional odds model should be interpreted cautiously. Although M3 demonstrated the best relative fit among the candidate models based on AIC and BIC, the violation of the proportional odds assumption represents an important limitation of the model.

### Assessment of Scale Effects

The scale effects were examined using the `scale_test()` function. The results indicated significant scale effects for Age, Gender, FAVC, FCVC, NCP, CAEC, CH2O, SCC, FAF, and TUE (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that the variability in the latent response may differ across levels of several predictors. Therefore, the results provide additional evidence that the standard proportional odds model may not fully capture the underlying structure of the data, and its coefficient estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

### Model Diagnostics

The diagnostic assessment identified substantial violations of the proportional odds assumption in the expanded ordinal logistic regression model (M3). The nominal effects test provided evidence of non-proportional effects for several predictors, suggesting that their associations with obesity levels were not consistent across the cumulative outcome thresholds.

The scale effects test also identified significant scale effects for several predictors, indicating potential differences in the variability of the latent response in relation to these predictors.

The supplementary multicollinearity assessment showed that adjusted GVIF values were generally close to 1, with no evidence of substantial multicollinearity among the predictors.

Although M3 demonstrated the lowest AIC and BIC among the candidate models, the violation of the proportional odds assumption limits the interpretation of its coefficients as common effects across all cumulative outcome thresholds. Therefore, M3 should be considered an exploratory reference model rather than a fully adequate proportional odds model.

# Discussion

This study examined the associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics and ordered obesity levels in the UCI dataset using cumulative link ordinal regression models.

The initial model comparison showed progressive improvements in model fit as demographic, dietary, and lifestyle predictors were added. Among the four candidate models, M3 had the lowest AIC and BIC, indicating the best relative fit among the models examined. However, improved model fit does not necessarily indicate that all model assumptions were satisfied.

Diagnostic analyses revealed substantial departures from the proportional odds assumption. The nominal effects test indicated non-proportional effects for several predictors, suggesting that their associations with obesity categories may differ across cumulative outcome thresholds. In addition, the scale effects test identified significant scale effects for most predictors, suggesting potential differences in the variability of the latent response in relation to several predictors. These findings indicate that the standard proportional odds model may not fully capture the relationships between the predictors and the ordered obesity outcome. Nevertheless, the supplementary VIF assessment indicated no substantial multicollinearity among the predictors.

Overall, the findings suggest that demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics were associated with obesity classification in the analyzed dataset. However, because of the diagnostic limitations of the expanded model, its coefficient estimates and odds ratios should be interpreted cautiously. Given the synthetic component of the dataset and the observational nature of the data, the findings should be considered exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships or generalized directly to the broader population.

## Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings.

First, the dataset contains both survey-based and synthetically generated observations. According to the UCI Machine Learning Repository, approximately 77% of the records were generated using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), while approximately 23% were collected through an online survey. Therefore, the dataset may not represent the broader population, and the findings should not be generalized without caution.

Second, diagnostic analyses identified substantial violations of the proportional odds assumption in the expanded ordinal logistic regression model. The nominal effects test indicated non-proportional effects for several predictors, suggesting that their associations with obesity categories may differ across cumulative outcome thresholds. In addition, the scale effects test identified significant scale effects for most predictors, suggesting that the variability of the latent response may differ in relation to several predictors. Although M3 showed the best relative fit according to AIC and BIC among the four candidate models, these diagnostic findings limit the interpretation of its coefficient estimates. Nevertheless, the supplementary VIF assessment did not indicate substantial multicollinearity among the predictors.

Third, some categorical predictor levels had very small frequencies. For example, the CALC variable contained only one observation in the Always category. Therefore, CALC was excluded from the primary dietary model to reduce potential estimation instability. Other variables, including SMOKE and MTRANS, were also not included in the final expanded model.

Finally, the observational nature of the dataset does not allow causal conclusions. The reported associations describe patterns within the available data and do not establish that specific dietary or lifestyle characteristics cause changes in obesity levels. 

The substantial proportion of synthetically generated records may also affect the estimated associations and statistical uncertainty. Therefore, the reported p-values and confidence intervals should be interpreted cautiously and may not reflect uncertainty in a representative population sample.

# Conclusion

This study examined the associations between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle characteristics and ordered obesity levels using ordinal logistic regression. Family history of overweight, age, dietary characteristics, physical activity, and technology use were associated with obesity categories in the analyzed dataset.

Among the four candidate models, M3 provided the best relative fit based on AIC and BIC. However, diagnostic analyses revealed substantial violations of the proportional odds assumption and significant scale effects for most predictors, limiting the interpretation of its estimates. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating model assumptions and data characteristics when analyzing ordinal outcomes.

Overall, the results provide an exploratory assessment of factors associated with obesity levels in the UCI dataset. Further research using representative population-based data and statistical models that accommodate non-proportional effects and scale heterogeneity is needed to obtain more reliable estimates of these associations.
